Bali:
The island of the gods

10 min read  ·  Seven-day itinerary

Bali is a Hindu island in a Muslim-majority archipelago, and that difference is visible everywhere: in the woven palm offerings set on every doorstep at dawn, in the split temple gates that frame village lanes, and in a ceremonial calendar so full that somewhere on the island a procession is always under way. Look past the beach clubs and it remains one of the most quietly devotional places on earth.

Why Bali?

Few islands pack so much variety into so small a space. In a single day you can climb from black-sand surf beaches through terraced rice valleys to cool crater lakes, passing tens of thousands of temples along the way. The Balinese subak — a thousand-year-old cooperative irrigation system run through water temples — is recognised by UNESCO, and it still shapes the green, stepped landscape of the interior.

Bali's popularity is real, and the south can feel crowded. The trick is to treat the island as several destinations: the surf and sunsets of the southwest, the craft villages and jungle ravines around Ubud, the volcanic highlands of the centre, and the slower, drier coasts of the north and east.

Practical tip

Traffic in southern Bali is heavy, and distances on the map are misleading — a 30 km trip can take two hours. Hire a driver by the day (typically IDR 600,000–800,000) rather than hopping between taxis, and base yourself in two or three areas instead of moving every night. Cover shoulders and knees at temples; a sarong and sash are required and usually available to borrow at the entrance.

The regions

Bali rewards slow movement between distinct areas. Ubud, the cultural heart, is the best base for dance performances, galleries, and rice-terrace walks. The Bukit Peninsula in the far south has dramatic limestone cliffs and world-class surf breaks. The central highlands around Bedugul and Munduk are cool, misty, and full of waterfalls, while Sidemen and Amed in the east feel decades removed from the resort strip.

Ubud

Bali's artistic centre — palace courtyards hosting nightly Legong and Kecak dances, woodcarving and silver villages nearby, and the Campuhan Ridge walk through elephant grass above two rivers.

Base for 3 nights

Uluwatu

A cliff-top temple on the Bukit Peninsula, 70 metres above the Indian Ocean. Stay for the sunset Kecak fire dance, then head down to the surf beaches of Padang Padang and Bingin.

Sunset essential

Sidemen

A quiet valley in the east framed by Mount Agung, Bali's sacred volcano. Rice paddies, songket weaving workshops, and guesthouses where the loudest sound is running water.

Slow travel favourite

Munduk

Highland village above the twin lakes of Buyan and Tamblingan, surrounded by clove and coffee plantations, rainforest trails, and some of the island's finest waterfalls.

Cool-weather escape

When to go

The dry season, from April to October, brings the most reliable weather, with July and August the busiest and most expensive months. The shoulder months of April–June and September are ideal: sunny days, greener landscapes, and far fewer visitors. The wet season (November–March) brings heavy afternoon downpours, but mornings are often clear and the rice fields are at their most vivid.

Plan around Nyepi, the Balinese Day of Silence (usually in March). For 24 hours the entire island shuts down — the airport closes, lights stay off, and visitors must remain in their hotels. The night before is loud and joyful, with giant ogoh-ogoh effigies paraded through every village.

Where to stay

Split your stay. Spend a few nights in or around Ubud — ideally in a villa or homestay in the surrounding villages of Penestanan, Sayan, or Keliki, rather than on the busy main road. Then move to the coast: Uluwatu for cliffs and surf, Sanur for a calm family-friendly beach, or Amed for snorkelling and diving off black-sand shores. Balinese family compounds offering homestay rooms are an excellent way to see daily ritual life up close.

Budget guide

Budget travellers in homestays and warungs can manage on IDR 500,000–800,000 (€30–45) per day. A comfortable mid-range trip — boutique hotel, a private driver some days, restaurant meals — runs IDR 1.5–3 million (€90–180) per day. Private pool villas start around IDR 2.5 million (€150) per night. Temple entry is usually IDR 30,000–75,000 (€2–5), and visitors pay a one-off tourist levy on arrival.

Food & drink

Balinese cooking is built on base genep, a fragrant spice paste of shallot, garlic, galangal, turmeric, and chilli. Look for babi guling (spit-roasted suckling pig), bebek betutu (duck slow-cooked in banana leaves), and lawar, a ceremonial mix of minced meat, coconut, and spices. The everyday staple is nasi campur — rice with a rotating spread of small dishes — best eaten at a local warung.

Bali also grows its own coffee in the Kintamani highlands, and Ubud's café scene is excellent. For something sweet, try dadar gulung (pandan crepes filled with palm sugar and coconut) from a morning market.
