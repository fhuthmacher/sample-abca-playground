Prague:
the city of a hundred spires

8 min read  ·  Four-day itinerary

Gothic towers, Baroque domes, Art Nouveau façades and Cubist lampposts all share the same few square kilometres along the Vltava. Prague came through the twentieth century with its old centre almost untouched, and walking it is like reading a thousand years of European architecture in one afternoon. Come early in the morning and stay late, because the city is at its best when the crowds have gone.

Why Prague?

Few capitals are as compact or as photogenic. From the Old Town Square, where the astronomical clock has marked the hours since 1410, it is a short walk across the Charles Bridge to Malá Strana, the “Lesser Town” of palaces and walled gardens, and then up the hill to Prague Castle, the largest ancient castle complex in the world. Every stretch of the route has another tower, another courtyard, another view across the red rooftops.

Prague is also a city of writers, composers and dissidents. Kafka was born here, Dvořák and Smetana wrote much of their music here, and Václav Havel's Velvet Revolution began in the streets around Wenceslas Square in 1989. Its culture is lively, wry and unpretentious: puppet theatres, jazz cellars, an excellent classical music scene and, of course, the best beer in the world at prices that still surprise visitors.

Practical tip

Cross the Charles Bridge before 7am or after 10pm to have its statues and views largely to yourself. Czechia uses the koruna (CZK), not the euro: pay by card where you can, avoid street exchange booths, and always choose to be charged in korunas when a card terminal offers a currency conversion.

The neighbourhoods

Staré Město (Old Town) is the medieval heart: a maze of lanes leading to the Old Town Square and the twin spires of the Týn Church. Next to it, Josefov holds the old Jewish Quarter, with six synagogues and the deeply affecting Old Jewish Cemetery. Across the river, Malá Strana sits below the castle hill, all Baroque palaces, embassies and quiet gardens. Nové Město (New Town), founded in 1348 and “new” only by Prague standards, runs along Wenceslas Square. For local life, head to Vinohrady and Žižkov, where leafy squares, wine bars and old-school pubs fill streets that few tour groups ever reach.

Prague Castle

A thousand years of Czech history on one hilltop: St. Vitus Cathedral with its Mucha stained glass, the Old Royal Palace, and Golden Lane's tiny painted houses, one of which Kafka once used as a writing room.

Half-day visit

Charles Bridge

Built in the 14th century and lined with thirty Baroque statues, this pedestrian bridge links the Old Town to Malá Strana. Climb the Old Town Bridge Tower for the classic view along the river toward the castle.

Early-morning walk

Old Town Square

Gothic, Baroque and Rococo façades surround the city's grandest square. Watch the astronomical clock's procession of apostles on the hour, then climb the Old Town Hall tower to look out over the Týn Church's spires.

Two-hour visit

Vyšehrad

A cliff-top fortress south of the centre with ramparts above the Vltava, the neo-Gothic Basilica of St. Peter and St. Paul, and the cemetery where Dvořák and Mucha are buried. It is calm, green, and free to wander.

Afternoon excursion

When to go

April to June and September to October bring mild weather (15–22°C), blossom or autumn colour on Petřín Hill, and slightly thinner crowds. The Prague Spring music festival fills concert halls from mid-May to early June. July and August are warm and very busy. Winter is cold, often below freezing, but atmospheric: the Christmas markets on the Old Town and Wenceslas squares run from late November, and a snowy Charles Bridge at dusk is unforgettable.

Where to stay

The Old Town puts the main sights on your doorstep, but it can be noisy and pricey. Malá Strana is quieter and more romantic, with boutique hotels in converted Baroque townhouses below the castle. For better value and a more local feel, stay in Vinohrady or Karlín: both are a few tram stops from the centre and full of cafés, bistros and parks. Prague's trams and metro are cheap, frequent and easy to use, so staying a little out of the centre costs you very little time.

Budget guide

Prague is still good value for a European capital, though prices in the historic centre have risen. Hostels start at around 500–800 CZK (€20–32) per night; mid-range hotels run 2,500–4,500 CZK (€100–180). A half-litre of draught beer costs 60–90 CZK in a neighbourhood pub, and a hearty lunch menu (polední menu) 180–260 CZK. A 72-hour public transport pass costs 330 CZK. Daily budget excluding accommodation: €40–70 for comfortable sightseeing.

Food & drink

Czech cooking is hearty and built for cold winters. Try svíčková (beef sirloin in a creamy root-vegetable sauce with bread dumplings and cranberries), pečená kachna (roast duck with red cabbage), and guláš served with a beer. Snack on chlebíčky, the colourful open sandwiches sold in delis, and skip the trdelník sold on every tourist street: it is a recent import, not a Czech tradition.

Beer is central to life here. Czechs drink more of it per person than anyone else in the world, and the pilsner style was invented an hour west of Prague, in Plzeň. Order a tankóvka (unpasteurised tank beer) in a traditional pub such as U Zlatého Tygra or U Fleků, or explore the city's growing craft scene. For something different, South Moravian wines are excellent, and the wine bars of Vinohrady pour them by the glass.
