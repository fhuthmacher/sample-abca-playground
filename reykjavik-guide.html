City  ·  Iceland

Reykjavík:
a small capital at the edge of the Arctic

8 min read  ·  Three-day itinerary

The world's northernmost capital is barely a city at all — a cluster of corrugated-iron houses painted red, yellow, and sky blue, gathered around a harbour beneath snow-capped Mount Esja. Yet Reykjavík punches far above its size: geothermal pools on every corner, a music and design scene out of all proportion to its population, and some of the most dramatic landscapes on Earth less than an hour's drive away.

Why Reykjavík?

Home to around 140,000 people, Reykjavík (“Smoky Bay”) takes its name from the steam that early Norse settlers saw rising from its hot springs in the 9th century. That same geothermal energy now heats almost every building in the city and fills its public swimming pools, which serve as Iceland's true social centres — places where locals soak in the hot pots and debate politics whatever the weather.

The city makes an ideal base for exploring Iceland. Within a day trip lie erupting volcanic landscapes, the tectonic rift of Þingvellir, thundering waterfalls, and black-sand beaches. And when you return in the evening, there is a surprisingly cosmopolitan city waiting: excellent seafood restaurants, intimate concert venues, and in winter, the chance of seeing the northern lights from the shoreline.

Practical tip

Keflavík International Airport is about 50 km from the city; the Flybus coach meets every arrival and takes around 45 minutes. The centre itself is compact and walkable — from Hallgrímskirkja down to the Old Harbour takes 15 minutes. Weather changes fast, so pack a waterproof shell and warm layers even in summer, and check road conditions at umferdin.is before any drive.

The landmarks

Hallgrímskirkja dominates the skyline: a soaring concrete church whose stepped façade was inspired by the basalt columns formed when lava cools. Take the lift to the top of the 74.5-metre tower for a view over the city's colourful rooftops to the mountains and the sea. From its steps, Skólavörðustígur — its pavement painted in rainbow stripes — leads downhill past galleries and wool shops toward the centre.

On the waterfront, Harpa concert hall glitters with a façade of geometric glass panels that catch the shifting Arctic light. A short walk east along the shore brings you to the Sun Voyager, a stainless-steel sculpture of a Viking-style ship facing out across the bay toward Mount Esja — at its most striking at sunset.

Hallgrímskirkja

Iceland's largest church and the city's defining landmark. The bright, austere interior houses a huge pipe organ, and the tower's viewing platform is the best way to get your bearings on the first day.

One-hour visit

Old Harbour & Grandi

Former fishing warehouses now home to seafood shacks, whale-watching departures, the Saga Museum, and the Reykjavík Maritime Museum. The Grandi food hall is a good stop for lunch.

Leisurely morning

Sundhöllin & the city pools

Reykjavík's oldest public pool, with a 1937 indoor hall and outdoor hot tubs. Shower thoroughly without a swimsuit before entering — it is the strict and non-negotiable local custom.

Evening soak

The Golden Circle

The classic day loop from the city: Þingvellir National Park, where the North American and Eurasian plates pull apart; the erupting Strokkur geyser; and the two-tiered Gullfoss waterfall.

Full-day trip

When to go

June through August brings the midnight sun, with nearly 24 hours of daylight, mild temperatures (10–15°C), open highland roads, and puffins nesting on islands just off the harbour. It is also the busiest and most expensive season. In June, festivals and long, bright evenings give the city a joyful energy.

September through March is northern-lights season. Days are short — around four hours of daylight in December — but dark skies offer the best chance of aurora, and the city glows with candlelit cafés and the New Year's Eve fireworks, which locals launch en masse across every neighbourhood. Shoulder months like September and April balance reasonable daylight with fewer crowds.

Where to stay

Base yourself in 101 Reykjavík, the central postcode around Laugavegur, the main shopping street, and the lanes beneath Hallgrímskirkja. Everything is walkable, and most day-tour companies offer hotel pickups from bus stops nearby. The Old Harbour area offers modern hotels with sea views, while the quieter residential streets of Vesturbær, west of the centre, have guesthouses close to the excellent Vesturbæjarlaug pool.

Budget guide

Reykjavík is one of Europe's most expensive cities. Hostel dorms start around ISK 6,000–9,000 (€40–60) per night; mid-range hotels run ISK 25,000–40,000 (€170–270). A main course at a mid-range restaurant costs ISK 4,000–6,000, and a pint of beer around ISK 1,500. Save by buying groceries at Bonús or Krónan, drinking the excellent tap water, and using happy hours. Card payments are accepted almost everywhere — you rarely need cash.

Food & drink

Icelandic cooking is built on the sea and the land: Arctic char, cod, and langoustine from the cold North Atlantic, and lamb that grazes freely on wild mountain herbs all summer. Try plokkfiskur, a comforting mash of fish and potato, or kjötsúpa, a hearty lamb soup. Skyr, the thick cultured dairy that Icelanders have eaten for over a thousand years, appears in everything from breakfast bowls to desserts.

For the city's most famous snack, queue at Bæjarins Beztu Pylsur, a harbourside hot-dog stand open since 1937 — order “eina með öllu” (one with everything) for crispy and raw onions, sweet mustard, ketchup, and remoulade. Adventurous eaters can sample hákarl, fermented shark, chased with a shot of BrennivBrénnivíniacute;n schnapps; most visitors try it once.
